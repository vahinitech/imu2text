How it works

1. The pen feels, it does not see

Sensors in the pen record how it moves, turns and presses, 100 times a second. Nothing records where the ink goes. The AI has to work out the letter from movement alone.

2. The AI learned from examples

It was shown about 20,000 letters with the right answer, and learned which movements go with which letter. Then it was tested on people it had never seen, which is the fair test for a new pen owner.

3. Small or capital is hard

A small o and a capital O are the same movement at a different size, and the pen barely feels size. 43% of the AI's mistakes are exactly this. Ignoring small and capital, it reads 84.3% right.

4. Five heads are better than one

Five copies of the AI were trained separately and vote together. Together they read 74.46% right; one alone reads 72.18% on average. When they disagree, it is a sign the writing is unusual.

Left-handed writing puzzles the runs

Why is it not 99%?

The pen cannot feel size

A small c and a capital C are the same movement, bigger. The pen measures speed and turning, not position, and capitals are also written faster, so the signals nearly match. 43.1% of the mistakes are this mix-up; ignoring small and capital, one CNN-BiLSTM reads 84.34% of letters from new people right instead of 72.46%.

New people write differently

Everyone has their own habits. For numbers and symbols, the same design reads 94.71% when it has seen the writer before and 71.03% for new people.

Few left-handed examples

Most training letters come from right-handed people. For new left-handed people the 5-run vote reads 59.47%, against 74.46% for right-handed people.

Words run together

In a word the model also has to find where each letter starts and ends. The word model here trained for only 15 rounds and reads 40.78% of words exactly.

Be careful with 99%

A model tested on the same people it learned from can score near 99% and still fail on a new writer. Every score on this page is on people the model never saw, unless it says "familiar people".

Questions to think about

These are open in this repo. Pick one, try it, and share what you find.

  1. The pen cannot feel size. What else could tell c from C? A capital often starts a word. Would reading the whole word help? Start: imu2text/seq2seq.py
  2. Left-handed people often push the pen where right-handed people pull it. Could flipped right-handed recordings work as left-handed practice data? Start: imu2text/augment.py
  3. When the 5 runs disagree, should an app ask the writer to write again? How much disagreement is too much? Start: the developer notes under step 3
  4. Which sensor matters most? Train without the magnetometer, or without the force sensor, and compare. Start: imu2text/models.py
  5. How many writers are enough? Does accuracy keep rising with every new person, or does it level off? Start: scripts/make_learning_curve.py
  6. Which cleaning filter would you design? Try it on step 2 and see if the AI reads better. Start: playground/stages.js

Why a CNN-BiLSTM? The other AI designs

The other ways to read a pen recording, what each scored, and why this page uses a CNN-BiLSTM. Open to read.

A pen recording is a short time series: 13 numbers, 100 times a second. Each design below reads it differently. Scores are letters from people the model never saw, unless the row says otherwise; "not measured" means nobody has run it on this data in this project yet.

Measured

CNN 48.7%*

Slides small windows along the signal and spots short patterns, like a quick upstroke.

Good when You need speed or a small chip.

CNN-BiLSTM 72.46%

The design on this page. The CNN finds the strokes, the BiLSTM joins them in order, attention picks the moments that matter.

Good when Little data, short signals. The design Ott et al. benchmark on OnHW.

Here 72.46% on the official test, 74.46% as a 5-run vote; 64.8%* on the left-handed split

LSTM, BiLSTM 43.8%* · 56.2%*

Reads the signal in time order and remembers what came before. A BiLSTM also reads it backwards.

Good when Order matters more than shape.

Next up

Transformer built

Every moment looks at every other moment through attention; no reading order.

Good when Much more data than OnHW has.

Mixture of experts built

Several small models, the experts, plus a router that sends each recording to the experts that suit it.

Good when Writers differ a lot, for example left- and right-handed.

Here built (build_moe), not measured

CNN-GRU not measured

A GRU is an LSTM with fewer parts. It learns the same kind of order.

Good when You want a smaller, faster model.

CNN-GNN no fair test yet

Treats the 13 sensors as connected points and learns how they relate.

Good when Sensors that move together carry the signal.

CNN-MLP not measured

After the CNN, a plain layer looks at everything at once and forgets the order.

Good when Very short, simple shapes.

Research

Autoencoder not measured

Learns to squeeze a recording and rebuild it, without any labels.

Good when You have many recordings but few with answers.

VAE or GAN not measured

Learns to make new, realistic recordings.

Good when A group is rare in the data, like left-handed people.

Hybrid quantum not measured

A small quantum circuit does part of the work, for example the filter of a CNN, and a normal network does the rest.

Good when Research only so far. Zaman et al. (2024) compared three such designs on 4 handwritten digits, 100 to 500 training images, on a quantum simulator.

DBN not measured

An older stack of layers trained one at a time, from before CNNs and LSTMs took over.

Good when Mostly of historical interest.

* Left-handed OnHW-chars_L, 2,270 letters, a split that shares writers between training and test, so these four are only comparable with each other. The official-test figures are right-handed OnHW-chars, 52 classes, seed 0. Details in docs/benchmarks.md.

Bigger is not better here

A larger BiLSTM (2 layers of 100) scored 99.2% on its own training letters and 70.4% on new people. It memorised. The smaller one with the same tricks did better on new people.

Combine, then vote

Training the same design 5 times and averaging lifts letters from new people from 72.18% (one run, on average) to 74.46%. A vote of different designs, say a CNN-BiLSTM and a Transformer, has not been tried.

Worth trying

A CNN in front of a Transformer, as speech models do. An autoencoder trained on unlabelled recordings first, then taught letters. A router that sends each recording to one of several small experts (build_moe is in the code, not measured).

Glossary

Plain meanings of the words on this page. Open to read.

Short, plain meanings of the words on this page, in five groups. The coloured row in each group is the word to know first. More detail in docs/glossary.md.

The pen
Sensor pen (IMU)
A pen with motion sensors inside. IMU means inertial measurement unit: sensors that feel movement.
Recording or sample
One letter, symbol or word as a table of sensor numbers over time.
Accelerometer
Feels speeding up, slowing down and gravity, in three directions (x, y, z). This pen has two, front and back.
Gyroscope
Feels how fast the pen turns.
Magnetometer
A compass: feels the Earth's magnetic field, which changes as the pen points another way.
Force sensor
Feels how hard the tip presses on the paper.
Filter
A way to clean a signal before the AI sees it, for example smoothing out fast wiggles.
The AI
Model or neural network
The AI: a program that learned from examples which movements mean which letter.
CNN-BiLSTM
The AI design used on this page. For letters and symbols: a CNN, then a BiLSTM, then attention. The word reader is a CNN and a BiLSTM.
CNN
The part of the model that spots short movement patterns, like a quick upstroke.
BiLSTM
The part that reads the movement in time order, forwards and backwards, to connect the strokes.
Attention
Lets the model weigh the moments of a recording that matter most.
GRU
A simpler LSTM. It remembers what came before with fewer parts, so it is smaller and faster.
Designs
Autoencoder
A model that learns to squeeze a recording and rebuild it. It learns without answers.
Transformer
A model where every moment of a recording looks at every other moment. It needs a lot of data.
VAE
Variational autoencoder: an autoencoder that can also make new, realistic recordings.
GNN
Graph neural network: treats things, here the 13 sensors, as points connected to each other.
Mixture of experts
A team of small models. A router picks which of them answers each recording.
Hybrid quantum network
A network where a small quantum circuit, today usually simulated on a normal computer, does one step.
Training
Training and testing
Training is learning from examples with answers. Testing is checking on examples the model never saw.
New people
Test writers who were not in the training data. Also called writer-independent. The fair test for a new pen owner.
Familiar people
The same people appear in training and test, so the model already knows their handwriting. Also called writer-dependent. Scores are higher.
Epoch or training round
One pass over all training letters. Training here runs 30.
Validation letters
Letters from training writers held back to check progress while training. The test people stay unseen.
Seed
The random starting point of training. The same design trained from 5 seeds gives CNN-BiLSTM #1 to #5.
Ensemble ("all 5 together")
Several models answer, and their scores are averaged into one answer.
Augmentation
Making extra training examples by slightly stretching or shaking real ones.
CTC
A training method that lets a model spell out a word without being told where each letter starts.
Scores
Confidence
How much of its score the model gives to an answer. 90% means it is quite sure.
Accuracy
The share of test samples read correctly.
Uncertainty
Two kinds. If the 5 models disagree, the writing is unlike what they learned from. If all hesitate between the same two answers, the movement itself fits both.
Calibration
Whether "80% sure" really means right 80% of the time. The calibration gap is how far off it is on average (ECE).
Entropy (bits)
How spread out the AI's scores are. 0 bits: all on one letter. Higher: it hesitates between several.
Disagreement (mutual information)
The part of the doubt that comes from the 5 runs giving different answers.
Confusion matrix
A grid of which letter was written (rows) and what the AI read (columns). Off the diagonal are mistakes.
Word list (lexicon)
A list of allowed words. The model scores every word on the list and picks the most likely one.
Character error rate (CER)
How many letters are wrong, as a share of the word's length. Lower is better.
